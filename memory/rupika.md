## Facts about markets, sources and tools I learned

- Package track technique that survives the gate: `[vertical] [city] family owned` or `locally owned`, plus BBB profiles naming the owner. It filled 12 of 12 on 2026-09-11 and 2026-09-12 with no Yelp figure at all. Complaint-language search (`"never called back"`) also works but skews toward bad contractors; check the license first.
- Metros that produced call-ready package prospects with that technique: Houston (5 hits, 5 verticals, best single metro), Oklahoma City, Tulsa, Nashville, Atlanta, Charlotte, Raleigh, Tampa, Orlando, Jacksonville, Miami, DC, Philadelphia, New York, Boston (each 3 per metro). Chicago, Detroit, Columbus, Indianapolis gave 9 of 12; Denver, Austin, Dallas gave 5 and Dallas was thinnest; San Diego, Portland, Seattle, Oakland gave 8; Las Vegas and Tucson gave 5.
- Metros that ran dry because advertisers answer in minutes and reviews praise their speed: Salt Lake City, Sacramento, San Jose, Albuquerque, Minneapolis. A straight re-run will not pay; they need a different discovery angle.
- Phoenix and LA are picked over for the package track after some 25 runs between 2026-08-10 and 2026-09-03; the LA Gateway Cities (Downey, Lawndale, Cerritos, Bellflower) and fresh Orange County suburbs outproduced Phoenix suburbs late on. Pest control in the Inland Empire was uniformly fast.
- As of 2026-09-12 the named flood-gates rotation in `_criteria.md` has been worked once end to end. Every metro still has untried suburb and vertical ground; the archive lists which combos were tried.
- Cross-suburb resolution is the default outcome, not a surprise: a suburb search surfaces businesses based in a neighbouring city. Record the confirmed home city and log the market by that, not by the search term.
- Verticals: electrical and restoration resolve worst from aggregated search results; HVAC, pest control, plumbing and painting resolve best. Supply houses and solar companies surface under contractor searches; check the trade before queuing.
- Website track: small markets of roughly 15k to 40k people are the sweet spot, and New England is underworked relative to the South, Midwest and Mountain West. Facebook-scoped search (`allowed_domains: facebook.com`) followed by a named "[Business] [city] website" search confirms the gap. Two misses per hit is normal; eight for three is not alarming in a fresh market. Productive recent markets: Hays KS, Morgantown WV, Watertown NY, Pendleton OR, Garden City KS, North Platte NE, Keene NH, Rutland VT, Augusta ME.
- Website-gap subtypes, all valid: no site beyond Facebook or directories; a dead or suspended domain; a free-builder subdomain (Ueniweb, Wix, GoDaddy, Google Sites); a manufacturer dealer-locator page as the only "site"; and the fastest confirmation of all, a directory or BBB "Website" field pointing at the business's Facebook page.
- Owner-name sources, best first: municipal or state license rosters (a city gas-fitters list, the City of Watertown master plumbers PDF, state contractor lookups), BBB principal contact, D&B managing member, the business's own Facebook About field, LinkedIn, then a personal-looking email (firstname@gmail) as a medium-confidence hint. An AI summary alone never names an owner.
- Emails: I record a listed email only when it appears on the business's own Facebook page, BBB or a directory listing. Emails and website fields that appear only in a search summary are unverified and are not written down. Most package reachability comes from a confirmed domain or phone plus owner name, not a listed email.
- Dedup rules that hold: anchor identity on address and phone, never on name; run a `-city -state` collision search on every hit, because "Affordable", "Quality", "Apex", "Total Lawn Care" and common surnames recur nationally; watch same-name towns in different states (Morgantown WV versus IN); grep this file and the archive for a market before calling it fresh; check `ls prospects/` and the hand-picked CSVs by name.
- Disqualifiers that have held across runs: franchise or network brand (Neighborly, Mr. Electric, SERVPRO, Paul Davis, CertaPro, Roto-Rooter, Overhead Door dealers); scale (400+ reviews, 11 to 50 employees, multi-location, a service area spanning double-digit cities or other states, "top X percent of contractors" rankings); a revoked, expired or missing license or an "Unlicensed Contractor" flag; BBB out of business or a deceased named owner; reviews that directly praise fast response; a pain figure that reads under one hour on a second check (an over-threshold drift is a flag, a sub-threshold reading is a drop); a single unverifiable source with no corroboration; and a character red flag such as threats toward a reviewer (Godfather Electric, Fast Plumbing), which outranks every other tier.
- Ads track (last run 2026-08-14): city-scoped `site:yelp.com [vertical] reviews [city]` surfaces review counts in snippets; 20 reviews was the working floor; a rating under about 3.5 is a drop; a small Yelp count beside hundreds of Birdeye reviews means paid marketing elsewhere.
- Tools: direct fetches to yelp.com and yellowpages.com return 403 or `EGRESS_BLOCKED`; the routine environment is on an allowlist, so WebFetch to most prospect domains fails too (Kevin was asked on 2026-09-03 to switch env `env_017Z1mtaA3sLwaPKW4fJPxZJ` to full network). One background sub-agent per track, each with file-write access and a brief that names the exclusions, runs in 6 to 9 minutes; commit each batch as it lands, since KIREEK commits mid-run and a rejected push only needs fetch and merge.
- Fresh containers usually start on a detached HEAD or a stale local `master`; `git fetch` then `git checkout -B master origin/master` fixes it every time. A stale `_fleet-state.md` does not block search-only work but gets flagged.
